Evolutionary Dynamics

Every checked strategy above starts with an equal share of the population. Each generation, strategies reproduce in proportion to how well they scored against the current population mix (replicator dynamics) — using the average payoffs from the tournament above.

Each generation, a small slice of the population is reshuffled uniformly across all strategies regardless of fitness — like a constant trickle of new individuals trying something different. Even a tiny rate can keep an extinct-looking strategy from ever truly hitting zero, letting it re-invade later if conditions change.

Spatial Evolutionary Dynamics

A completely different mechanism from the population mixing above: strategies live on a grid and only ever interact with their 8 neighbors. Each generation, every cell plays a short match against each neighbor, then copies whichever strategy (its own or a neighbor's) scored highest nearby. Spatial structure lets cooperation survive in clusters even in situations where a well-mixed population couldn't sustain it at all.

How this works

Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma: two players repeatedly choose to Cooperate or Defect without communicating. Defecting always scores at least as well as cooperating in a single round, but mutual cooperation beats mutual defection — the tension between individual and collective incentive is the whole point of the game.

Tournament: every strategy plays every strategy (including itself) for a fixed number of rounds; scores accumulate round by round using the payoff matrix above. This recreates Robert Axelrod's famous 1980s computer tournaments, where the simple Tit for Tat (cooperate first, then copy whatever the opponent just did) repeatedly beat much more elaborate strategies.

Noise: with noise > 0, a strategy's intended move is occasionally flipped — simulating miscommunication or execution error. This tends to hurt unforgiving strategies like Grim Trigger (one accidental defection locks in permanent retaliation) more than forgiving ones like Pavlov or Tit for Two Tats.

Evolutionary dynamics: instead of one tournament, imagine a population where each strategy's share grows or shrinks based on its average payoff against the current population — better-than-average strategies grow, worse-than-average strategies shrink toward extinction. This is the same replicator-dynamics idea used across evolutionary biology and economics, applied to Axelrod's original strategy set. A small mutation rate keeps a constant trickle of every strategy in the mix regardless of fitness, so nothing is ever permanently and completely wiped out.

Beyond the Prisoner's Dilemma: the payoff matrix presets switch between classic 2x2 symmetric games by reordering the same four values. Prisoner's Dilemma (T>R>P>S) rewards defection individually even though mutual cooperation is better for both. Stag Hunt (R>T>P>S) makes mutual cooperation the single best outcome, but trusting your partner is risky. Chicken (T>R>S>P) makes mutual defection the worst outcome for both — better to back down than crash together. Deadlock (T>P>R>S) removes the dilemma entirely: defecting is just better, period.

Play it yourself: the "Play Against a Strategy Yourself" panel lets you personally face off against any strategy, one click at a time, under whatever payoff matrix is currently active — the fastest way to feel why Tit for Tat is so hard to exploit, or why Stag Hunt tempts you to defect even when you'd rather cooperate.

Spatial dynamics: the population-mixing evolution above assumes every strategy can meet every other strategy with a probability equal to its population share — a "well-mixed" population. Real populations aren't like that: who you interact with depends on who's near you. The spatial grid models this directly — each cell only ever plays its 8 neighbors, and copies the best-performing strategy in its immediate neighborhood (including itself) each generation. The default invader scenario (a single Always Defect cell dropped into a sea of Always Cooperate) shows exactly why spatial structure matters: that lone defector's neighbors get repeatedly exploited, so it looks like the best strategy nearby and gets copied — and the defection spreads outward generation by generation until it dominates almost the entire grid, something a single defector could never do starting from a tiny share of a well-mixed population above. The reverse experiment is just as informative: try a single Tit for Tat cell dropped into a sea of Always Defect instead, and watch it get wiped out in one generation — an isolated nice strategy with no others nearby to cooperate with has no way to outscore its exploitative neighbors. Real invasion of a defector-dominated space generally needs a whole cluster of cooperators arriving together, not a lone individual.
